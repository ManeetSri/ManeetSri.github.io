## Why it matters

Part VI has given you more than twenty graph algorithms. In an interview or a contest, nobody tells you which one to use: the problem talks about flights, friendships, pipes or chores, and you have to see the graph and then pick the tool. Picking well comes down to three questions, asked in order: **what are the vertices and edges, what exactly is being asked, and how big is the input?** This lesson turns Part VI into a decision table, a flowchart for the most common family, and a list of the clues problem statements leave.

## Step 1: find the graph

Many graph problems never say "graph". Before choosing an algorithm, name the pieces:

- **Vertices** are the things: cities, people, grid cells, words, whole states of a puzzle.
- **Edges** are the relationships or moves: roads, "follows", stepping to a neighbouring cell, changing one letter.
- Decide whether edges are **directed** (one-way: dependencies, follows, flights) or **undirected** (two-way: friendships, roads), and whether they have **weights** (distances, costs, capacities).

Grids and puzzles are **implicit graphs**, as in [Implicit graphs: grids and state spaces](/dsa/learn/graph-implicit): you generate the neighbours on the fly instead of storing edges. Once the pieces are named, the question usually names its algorithm.

## The shortest-path flowchart

Shortest paths are the family people get wrong most often, because several algorithms give correct answers and only one is the right speed. Ask about the shape of the graph, then the weights:


```text
Shortest paths from ONE source:

  Is the graph a DAG?             yes → topological order + DP      O(V + E)
    no ↓
  Do all edges cost the same?     yes → BFS                         O(V + E)
    no ↓
  Are the only weights 0 and 1?   yes → 0-1 BFS                     O(V + E)
    no ↓
  Is any weight negative?         no  → Dijkstra (A* for one target) O((V + E) log V)
    yes ↓
  Bellman–Ford, which also reports negative cycles                  O(V · E)

Shortest paths between EVERY pair:

  Is V at most about 400?         yes → Floyd–Warshall              O(V³)
    no → run the one-source choice above from every vertex
```

[Choosing a shortest-path algorithm](/dsa/learn/sp-choosing) covers this in more depth, including when [A* search](/dsa/learn/sp-a-star) pays off.

## Clues in the wording

| The statement says… | Think |
| --- | --- |
| "minimum number of moves / steps / swaps" | BFS over states |
| "prerequisites", "must come before", "build order" | topological sort |
| "connect all … at minimum cost" | MST |
| "groups", "merge", "are they connected yet?", edges added over time | union-find |
| "mutually reachable", "everyone in the group can reach everyone" | SCC |
| "critical", "single point of failure", "removing this disconnects" | bridges, articulation points |
| "use every ticket / domino / edge exactly once" | Euler path |
| "visit every city exactly once", with n ≤ 20 | bitmask DP |
| "capacity", "throughput", "as many as possible through" | max flow |
| "assign each … to at most one …" | bipartite matching |
| "ancestor", "common manager", "distance in a tree", many queries | LCA |
| "each item is one of two options", with "if … then …" rules | 2-SAT |

## Let the constraints choose

The input sizes often decide between two plausible algorithms. Using the rule of thumb from [From n to a feasible complexity](/dsa/learn/bigo-feasibility-table), about a hundred million simple steps per second:

| Size | What fits |
| --- | --- |
| V ≤ 16–20 | exponential in V: bitmask DP over subsets of vertices |
| V ≤ 400–500 | O(V³): Floyd–Warshall, matrix-based flow |
| V, E ≤ 10⁵–10⁶ | O((V + E) log V) or better: BFS, DFS, Dijkstra, MST, SCC, bridges, LCA |
| huge, or implicit and endless | BFS or A* that stops at the target, never storing the whole graph |

A problem with V ≤ 15 is almost shouting "bitmask". A problem with 10⁵ vertices and 10⁵ queries on a tree rules out anything O(n) per query.

<Callout kind="gotcha" title="Traps worth checking for">
- **Dijkstra with negative weights** gives wrong answers, silently. Check the constraints for negative values.
- **DFS for shortest paths** finds *a* path, not the shortest. Unweighted shortest paths are BFS.
- **Undirected and directed cycles are different questions.** Undirected: DFS with a parent check, or union-find. Directed: three-colour DFS or Kahn's algorithm, as in [Cycle detection](/dsa/learn/gt-cycles).
- **Union-find can't delete edges.** If edges disappear over time, process the operations in reverse so they become additions, or use another structure.
- **Deep recursion.** Any DFS-based algorithm (components, topological sort, SCC, bridges) recurses once per vertex along a path. With 10⁵ vertices inside a test, use an explicit stack.
- **"Every vertex once" is not "every edge once".** Euler paths are O(E); Hamiltonian paths are NP-hard. Read the statement twice.
</Callout>
