## Why it matters

A sawmill charges by the length of the board it puts through the saw. A video editor splitting a long recording re-encodes the whole chunk it's working on at each split. Whenever the cost of an operation depends on the size of the piece you apply it to, the **order** of operations matters. Minimum cost to cut a stick is the cleanest example. It's interval DP again, after one transformation of the input, and it shows that "choose what happens first" works perfectly well when the cost depends only on the range.

## The problem

A stick has length L, with marks at some positions where it must be cut. Cutting a piece costs **the length of that piece**, whatever its length is at that moment. You can make the cuts in any order. What's the cheapest total?

Take a stick of length 10 with marks at 2, 4 and 7:

| Order | Costs | Total |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2, 4, 7 | 10 (the whole stick), then 8 (the piece from 2 to 10), then 6 (from 4 to 10) | 24 |
| 7, 4, 2 | 10, then 7 (from 0 to 7), then 4 (from 0 to 4) | 21 |
| 4, 2, 7 | 10, then 4 (from 0 to 4), then 6 (from 4 to 10) | **20** |

## Middle first isn't always best

A natural guess is to cut nearest the middle, so the pieces shrink fast. It's often good, but not always. With length 10 and marks at 4, 5 and 6:

- **Middle first:** cut at 5 for 10. Then the piece from 0 to 5 is cut at 4 for 5, and the piece from 5 to 10 at 6 for 5. Total **20**.
- **Better:** cut at 4 for 10. The piece from 4 to 10 is cut at 6 for 6, and the piece from 4 to 6 at 5 for 2. Total **18**.

The marks are bunched together, and it pays to cut off the bunch first and do the fiddly cuts on a short piece. No simple local rule captures that, so it's DP. [When greedy fails](/dsa/learn/greedy-counterexamples) collects more traps of this kind.

## Turning marks into ranges

Two small steps make this look like the earlier lessons:

1. **Sort the marks.** The input may list them in any order.
2. **Add both ends** of the stick as points: `points = [0] + sorted marks + [L]`.

Now every piece that ever exists runs from one point to another, `points[left]` to `points[right]`, with the marks strictly between them still to be cut. That's a range of indexes into `points`, exactly like the walls in [Burst balloons](/dsa/learn/dpi-burst-balloons).

> `best[left][right]` is the cheapest way to make every cut strictly between `points[left]` and `points[right]`, starting from that one piece.

## This time, choose the first cut

Which choice splits a piece into independent parts? Look at the **first** cut made on it, at some point k between `left` and `right`:

- It costs the piece's length, `points[right] - points[left]`, which the range itself tells you. Nothing that happens later can change that.
- Afterwards there are two separate pieces, from `left` to k and from k to `right`. Cutting one never changes the other.

So:

> `best[left][right] = (points[right] - points[left]) + min over left < k < right of best[left][k] + best[k][right]`

with `best[left][left + 1] = 0`, since a piece with no marks inside needs no cuts. The answer is `best[0][m - 1]`, where m is the number of points.

Why does "first" work here when it failed for balloons? A balloon's score depends on its neighbours **when it's popped**, and the first pop changes other balloons' neighbours, tangling the two sides together. A cut's cost depends only on the piece's two ends, which the range already fixes, and the two pieces never interact again. The general rule: **choose the decision whose cost the range determines, and after which the two sides are independent.** Sometimes that's the first operation, sometimes the last.

```swift run
func cheapestCutting(length: Int, marks: [Int]) -> (cost: Int, order: [Int]) {
    let points = [0] + marks.sorted() + [length]        // sort, then add both ends
    let m = points.count
    var best = Array(repeating: Array(repeating: 0, count: m), count: m)
    var firstCut = Array(repeating: Array(repeating: -1, count: m), count: m)
    if m >= 3 {
        for gap in 2..<m {                              // shorter pieces first
            for left in 0..<(m - gap) {
                let right = left + gap
                best[left][right] = Int.max
                for k in (left + 1)..<right where best[left][k] + best[k][right] < best[left][right] {
                    best[left][right] = best[left][k] + best[k][right]
                    firstCut[left][right] = k
                }
                best[left][right] += points[right] - points[left]   // the first cut's cost, added once
            }
        }
    }

    // The first cut of a piece, then everything on its left piece, then its right piece
    func order(_ left: Int, _ right: Int) -> [Int] {
        guard right - left >= 2 else { return [] }
        let k = firstCut[left][right]
        return [points[k]] + order(left, k) + order(k, right)
    }

    return (best[0][m - 1], order(0, m - 1))
}

print(cheapestCutting(length: 10, marks: [7, 2, 4]))
print(cheapestCutting(length: 10, marks: [4, 5, 6]))
print(cheapestCutting(length: 9, marks: [5, 1, 6, 3]))
print(cheapestCutting(length: 5, marks: []))
```

```output
(cost: 20, order: [4, 2, 7])
(cost: 18, order: [4, 6, 5])
(cost: 21, order: [3, 1, 6, 5])
(cost: 0, order: [])
```

What to notice:

- The length of the piece is added **once**, after the k loop, because every choice of k pays it. Adding it inside the loop would also work but does more arithmetic.
- The work depends on the number of marks, not on L. With 100 marks on a stick a billion units long, the table is 102 × 102. Indexing by position would need a table of size L, which is hopeless.
- `order` lists cuts in a valid order: each piece's first cut comes before any cut inside its two halves.

## Cost

With m marks there are m + 2 points, about m²/2 ranges and up to m choices each: **O(m³)** time and **O(m²)** memory, plus O(m log m) for sorting. A few hundred marks is fine.

## Read backwards: merging neighbours

Run the cuts in reverse and they become **merges**: start with the final pieces and repeatedly glue two **adjacent** pieces together, paying the combined length. The cheapest cutting plan and the cheapest merging plan are the same plan, reversed.

If any two pieces could be merged, not just neighbours, this would be Huffman's problem, and the greedy "merge the two smallest" from [Huffman coding](/dsa/learn/greedy-huffman) would solve it. Requiring neighbours breaks the greedy, because the two smallest pieces might not be next to each other. That restriction is exactly what makes it interval DP. Problems about merging adjacent piles of stones at a cost are this same DP.

<Callout kind="gotcha" title="Common mistakes">
- **Forgetting to sort the marks.** The ranges only make sense when points are in order along the stick.
- **Forgetting the ends.** Without 0 and L in `points`, the first cut has no piece length to charge.
- **Making the table L by L.** Index by marks, not positions.
- **Thinking "first cut" means a greedy choice.** The DP tries every first cut for every piece. It only fixes *which kind* of decision splits the range.
</Callout>
