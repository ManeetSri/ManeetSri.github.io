## Why it matters

These are the two textbook greedy algorithms, and they belong together because they show the difference between a problem greedy solves and one it doesn't. **Activity selection** fits as many bookings as possible into one room. The **fractional knapsack** fills a bag with the most valuable mix of goods you can split, like spices at a market or bandwidth shared between video streams. Its cousin, the 0/1 knapsack, where items can't be split, looks almost identical and defeats every greedy rule. Knowing why is the whole point.

## Activity selection

A recording studio takes bookings. Each booking is a half-open interval of hours, and two bookings can't overlap, though one may start the moment another ends. Accept as many as possible.

You've met the rule twice: in [Non-overlapping intervals and arrows](/dsa/learn/iv-non-overlapping) and in [Proving greedy](/dsa/learn/greedy-proofs), which proved it both ways. **Sort by end time, and accept each booking that starts at or after the last accepted one ends.**

```swift run
let bookings = [
    (name: "podcast", start: 9, end: 11),
    (name: "choir", start: 10, end: 12),
    (name: "drums", start: 11, end: 13),
    (name: "voiceover", start: 9, end: 10),
    (name: "band", start: 12, end: 16),
    (name: "strings", start: 13, end: 14),
    (name: "audiobook", start: 14, end: 17),
]

var accepted: [String] = []
var freeFrom = Int.min                       // when the studio is next free
for booking in bookings.sorted(by: { $0.end < $1.end }) where booking.start >= freeFrom {
    accepted.append(booking.name)
    freeFrom = booking.end
}
print(accepted)
```

```output
["voiceover", "choir", "strings", "audiobook"]
```

The voiceover ends first, at 10, so it goes in. The choir starts exactly at 10, so it fits. The drums start at 11, before the choir ends at 12, so they're turned away. The band starts at 12 but runs to 16, and by then the strings, which end at 14, have taken the slot. Step through the same bookings:

```visual intervals

input:
  intervals: [[9, 11], [10, 12], [11, 13], [9, 10], [12, 16], [13, 14], [14, 17]]
```

The visualiser counts the bookings turned away (3); the accepted ones are the other 4.

### The mirror image works too

Sorting by end and sweeping forwards through time is one way. Sorting by **start, latest first**, and sweeping backwards works equally well: accept each booking that ends at or before the earliest start accepted so far. It's the same problem with time running backwards, and the same proof applies. What doesn't work is earliest start, shortest first or fewest overlaps first: each has a counterexample.

### When bookings have values

Suppose each booking pays a different amount and you want the most money, not the most bookings. A booking from 0 to 10 paying 100 against two bookings, 0 to 5 and 5 to 10, paying 1 each: earliest end takes the two small ones and earns 2. No sort order fixes this, because the right choice depends on adding up values across combinations. That's **weighted interval scheduling**, and it needs dynamic programming (from M51).

## The fractional knapsack

A trader at a spice market has a bag that holds 10 kg. The stall has 4 kg of tea worth 20 in total, 6 kg of coffee worth 18, 5 kg of cocoa worth 35 and 8 kg of sugar worth 8. Anything can be scooped by the kilogram, so the trader can take part of a sack, at the same price per kilogram. What's the most valuable bagful?

The rule: **value density**, value per kilogram, highest first. Take all of the densest good; when the next one doesn't fit whole, take as much of it as fits, and stop.

```swift run
struct Item {
    let name: String
    let weight: Int        // kilograms available
    let value: Int         // the value of all of it
}

// Fractional knapsack: densest first, and a fraction of the first item that doesn't fit
func bestLoad(_ items: [Item], capacity: Int) -> Double {
    // Higher value per kg first; a.value / a.weight > b.value / b.weight, without dividing
    let densestFirst = items.sorted { $0.value * $1.weight > $1.value * $0.weight }
    var room = capacity
    var total = 0.0
    for item in densestFirst where room > 0 {
        let take = min(item.weight, room)
        let worth = Double(item.value) * Double(take) / Double(item.weight)
        print("\(take) kg of the \(item.weight) kg of \(item.name): \(worth)")
        total += worth
        room -= take
    }
    return total
}

let stall = [
    Item(name: "tea", weight: 4, value: 20),
    Item(name: "coffee", weight: 6, value: 18),
    Item(name: "cocoa", weight: 5, value: 35),
    Item(name: "sugar", weight: 8, value: 8),
]
print("total", bestLoad(stall, capacity: 10))
```

```output
5 kg of the 5 kg of cocoa: 35.0
4 kg of the 4 kg of tea: 20.0
1 kg of the 6 kg of coffee: 3.0
total 58.0
```

Cocoa is worth 7 per kg, tea 5, coffee 3 and sugar 1. Cocoa and tea fill 9 kg, and the last kilogram is coffee, worth 18 ÷ 6 = 3. Sugar never makes it in. Sorting dominates: O(n log n), then one pass.

### Why density is right: exchange a kilogram

Here's the exchange argument. Take any best bagful, and suppose it holds **less** of the densest good than it could, while holding some kilogram of a less dense good. Swap that kilogram for a kilogram of the densest good. The weight is the same, and the value goes up, or stays the same if the densities are equal. So there's a best bagful that takes as much of the densest good as possible: either all of it, or enough to fill the bag. That's the greedy's first choice. What's left is the same problem with a smaller bag and one fewer good, so by induction every choice is safe.

The swap only works because you can trade a **little** weight for the same little weight. Hold on to that.

### Why the 0/1 knapsack breaks it

In [When greedy fails](/dsa/learn/greedy-counterexamples) a 10 kg bag met a 6 kg lamp worth 30 and a 5 kg rug and a 5 kg vase worth 20 each. With splitting allowed, the density rule takes the lamp and 4 kg of the rug, worth 30 + 16 = 46, and that's optimal. Without splitting, it takes the lamp and then nothing fits, for 30, while the rug and vase give 40.

The exchange argument fails at exactly one step. To make room for more of the lamp, you'd have to swap out a **whole** item, and the lamp and the item you remove don't weigh the same. The swap can leave a gap that nothing fills. Once trades of "a little" aren't allowed, density no longer decides the answer.

| | Fractional | 0/1 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Items | can be split | whole or nothing |
| Algorithm | greedy by value density | dynamic programming |
| Time | O(n log n) | O(n × capacity) |
| The exchange | swap a kilogram for a kilogram | breaks: whole items weigh different amounts |

You'll solve the 0/1 version in [0/1 knapsack, from 2D to 1D](/dsa/learn/dpk-01).

<Callout kind="gotcha" title="Swift gotcha: integer division ruins densities">
With `Int` values, `item.value / item.weight` throws away the remainder: 7 ÷ 2 becomes 3 and 8 ÷ 3 becomes 2, so items with different densities can tie or even swap order. Either convert first, `Double(item.value) / Double(item.weight)`, or compare without dividing: a is denser than b exactly when `a.value * b.weight > b.value * a.weight`. The cross-multiplication is exact, as long as the products fit in an `Int`.
</Callout>
