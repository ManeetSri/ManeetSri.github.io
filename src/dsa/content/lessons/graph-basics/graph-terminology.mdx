## Why it matters

A map app finding a route, a social network suggesting friends, Swift Package Manager deciding which target to build first, Xcode working out which screens can push which: all of these are **graphs**, things connected to other things. Graphs are the most general structure in this course. Lists, trees and grids are all special cases, and a huge share of interview problems turn into a graph problem once you see the connections. Before any algorithm, you need the vocabulary, because every graph lesson and problem statement uses it without explaining it again.

## Vertices and edges

A graph has two ingredients:

- **Vertices** (also called **nodes**): the things. Cities, people, files, courses.
- **Edges**: the connections between pairs of vertices. Roads, friendships, imports, prerequisites.

Here's a small graph of five friends. Each line is an edge:

```text
   Ana ---- Ben ---- Dev
     \      /         |
      \    /          |
       Cleo          Eli
```

Ana is friends with Ben and Cleo, Ben with Ana, Cleo and Dev, and Dev with Ben and Eli. That's 5 vertices and 5 edges. When people talk about graph algorithms they write **V** for the number of vertices and **E** for the number of edges (problem statements often say n and m instead). Costs are given in both: "O(V + E)" means the work grows with the number of vertices plus the number of edges.

Two vertices joined by an edge are **adjacent**, or **neighbours**. Ben's neighbours are Ana, Cleo and Dev.

You've met graphs already. A tree, from [Tree terminology](/dsa/learn/tree-terminology), is a graph where the edges run from parents to children and there's exactly one route between any two nodes. Everything you learnt about trees still holds; graphs remove the restrictions.

## Directed and undirected

Friendship goes both ways: if Ana is Ben's friend, Ben is Ana's. An edge like that is **undirected**, and a graph of them is an undirected graph.

Following on a social network doesn't: you can follow someone who doesn't follow you back. That's a **directed** edge, drawn as an arrow from the follower to the followed, and a graph of them is a **directed graph** (or **digraph**):

```text
   you ---> swiftlang ---> apple
    ^          |
    |          v
   sam <---- dana
```

Here you follow swiftlang, swiftlang follows apple and dana, dana follows sam, and sam follows you. Directed edges have a start and an end. "Is there an edge from u to v?" and "is there an edge from v to u?" are different questions.

Real systems are full of both kinds:

| Undirected | Directed |
| --- | --- |
| roads you can drive either way | one-way streets |
| friendships | follows, links between web pages |
| cables in a network | imports between Swift files, prerequisites between courses |

## Weighted edges

Sometimes an edge carries a number: a road's length, a flight's price, a cable's delay. That number is the edge's **weight**, and the graph is **weighted**. A graph without weights is **unweighted**, which is the same as every edge having weight 1. The difference matters: in an unweighted graph the shortest route uses the fewest edges, but in a weighted one, three short roads can beat one long road. Shortest paths in weighted graphs get a module of their own, starting with [Weighted graphs and the shortest-path problem](/dsa/learn/sp-weighted).

## Degree

The **degree** of a vertex in an undirected graph is the number of edges touching it. In the friends graph, Ben has degree 3 and Eli has degree 1.

In a directed graph you count the two directions separately:

- **in-degree**: edges arriving at the vertex (how many follow you);
- **out-degree**: edges leaving it (how many you follow).

Counting degrees from a list of edges is a dictionary job, as in [Counting with default: and grouping(by:)](/dsa/learn/swift-counting-grouping):

```swift run
// Undirected: each friendship touches two people
let friendships = [("Ana", "Ben"), ("Ana", "Cleo"), ("Ben", "Cleo"), ("Ben", "Dev"), ("Dev", "Eli")]
var degree: [String: Int] = [:]
for (a, b) in friendships {
    degree[a, default: 0] += 1
    degree[b, default: 0] += 1
}
for person in degree.keys.sorted() {
    print(person, degree[person]!)
}
print("sum of degrees:", degree.values.reduce(0, +), "edges:", friendships.count)

// Directed: each follow leaves one account and arrives at another
let follows = [("you", "swiftlang"), ("swiftlang", "apple"), ("swiftlang", "dana"), ("dana", "sam"), ("sam", "you")]
var inDegree: [String: Int] = [:]
var outDegree: [String: Int] = [:]
for (from, to) in follows {
    outDegree[from, default: 0] += 1
    inDegree[to, default: 0] += 1
}
for account in ["you", "swiftlang", "apple", "dana", "sam"] {
    print(account, "in:", inDegree[account, default: 0], "out:", outDegree[account, default: 0])
}
```

```output
Ana 2
Ben 3
Cleo 2
Dev 2
Eli 1
sum of degrees: 10 edges: 5
you in: 1 out: 1
swiftlang in: 1 out: 2
apple in: 1 out: 0
dana in: 1 out: 1
sam in: 1 out: 1
```

What to notice:

- **The sum of the degrees is twice the number of edges.** Every undirected edge has two ends, so it adds 1 to two degrees. This is called the handshake lemma, and it's a quick sanity check on any code that builds an undirected graph.
- In a directed graph, the in-degrees add up to E, and so do the out-degrees, because every edge leaves exactly one vertex and arrives at exactly one.
- The dictionary is printed through sorted keys, or a fixed list, never directly: a dictionary's order changes from run to run.

In-degree and out-degree alone answer real questions. A vertex with in-degree 0 has nothing pointing at it: in a prerequisite graph, it's a course you can take straight away. That idea is the whole of [Kahn's algorithm](/dsa/learn/topo-kahn), two lessons into M43.

## Paths, cycles and connectivity

A **path** is a sequence of vertices where each one is adjacent to the next: Ana → Ben → Dev → Eli. Its **length** is the number of edges, here 3. In a directed graph the path must follow the arrows. A **simple** path never repeats a vertex.

A **cycle** is a path that returns to where it started without reusing an edge: Ana → Ben → Cleo → Ana. The follows graph has the cycle you → swiftlang → dana → sam → you. A graph with no cycles is **acyclic**.

An undirected graph is **connected** if there's a path between every pair of vertices. If not, it falls into separate pieces called **connected components**: a network with two islands that no bridge joins has two components. Finding them is a lesson of its own, [Connected components and flood fill](/dsa/learn/gt-components).

Directed graphs need a stronger idea. A directed graph is **strongly connected** if you can get from every vertex to every other **following the arrows**. The follows graph isn't: apple follows nobody, so no path starts at apple. Its strongly connected pieces are the four accounts on the cycle, which can all reach each other, and apple on its own. If you ignore the arrows and the graph is in one piece, it's called **weakly** connected.

## Special kinds of graph

A few shapes come up so often that they have names.

**DAG** (directed acyclic graph): directed, with no cycles. Dependencies form DAGs: if target A needs B and B needs A, nothing could ever be built. M43 is all about DAGs.

**Tree**: a connected, undirected graph with no cycles. It always has exactly V − 1 edges, and there's exactly one path between any two vertices. Choosing one vertex as the root gives the parent-and-child trees of Part V.

**Bipartite**: the vertices split into two groups, with every edge going between the groups, never inside one. Students and the clubs they belong to, or job applicants and jobs. Checking whether a graph is bipartite is [Bipartite check](/dsa/learn/gt-bipartite).

**Self-loops and multi-edges**: a **self-loop** is an edge from a vertex to itself, like a web page linking to itself. **Multi-edges** (or parallel edges) are two or more edges between the same pair, like two different roads between the same towns. A graph with neither is **simple**. Most problems promise a simple graph; when they don't, you'll need to decide what a repeated edge means (keep the cheapest? count both?), which [Dense vs sparse, and reading graph input](/dsa/learn/graph-dense-sparse) covers.

## Sparse and dense

A simple undirected graph with V vertices can have at most V(V − 1)/2 edges, every pair joined; a directed one at most V(V − 1). A graph with close to that many edges is **dense**. One with far fewer, closer to V than to V², is **sparse**.

Most real graphs are sparse. A road network has millions of junctions, but each joins only three or four roads. You have hundreds of friends, not billions. This shapes everything: an algorithm that costs O(V + E) on a sparse graph is close to O(V), while one that costs O(V²) is hopeless for a million vertices whatever E is. The next lesson shows how the choice of representation follows from this.

<Callout kind="gotcha" title="The classic slips">
- **Counting an undirected edge once in the degrees.** Each undirected edge adds 1 to the degree of **both** its ends. If your degree total isn't 2E, an end went missing.
- **Mixing up in and out.** In-degree counts arrows arriving. If an edge `a → b` means "a must happen before b", then b's in-degree is the number of things b waits for.
- **Assuming connected.** "Visit every vertex" from one start misses the other components. Many graph bugs come from a test case with two separate pieces.
</Callout>

## Practice

**The trusted archivist** is pure in-degree and out-degree: find the vertex that everyone points at and that points at nobody, using nothing but two arrays of counts.

  back: "V is the number of vertices and E the number of edges. O(V + E) means the work grows with vertices plus edges, the usual cost of visiting a whole graph once."
- id: graph-handshake
  front: "In an undirected graph, what do all the degrees add up to?"
  back: "2E, twice the number of edges, because every edge adds 1 to the degree of each of its two ends."
- id: graph-in-out-degree
  front: "What are in-degree and out-degree?"
  back: "In a directed graph, in-degree counts the edges arriving at a vertex and out-degree the edges leaving it."

  front: "What is a DAG, and where do you meet one?"
  back: "A directed acyclic graph: directed edges and no cycles. Dependency graphs, such as build targets or course prerequisites, are DAGs."
