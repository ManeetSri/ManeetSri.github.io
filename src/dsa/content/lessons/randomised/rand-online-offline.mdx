## Why it matters

Most algorithms in this course see their whole input before deciding anything. Real systems often can't. A cache must decide what to evict **now**, without knowing which item will be requested next. A ride-hailing app assigns a driver before it knows where the next rider will appear. An autoscaler decides whether to add servers before it knows whether a traffic spike will last. These are **online** problems, and for them "the best possible answer" isn't on the table: you'd need to see the future. Instead you ask how much worse than perfect hindsight your strategy can be, in the worst case. That number, the **competitive ratio**, is how online algorithms are judged, and it explains why caches use the policies they do, the subject of the next lesson.

## Offline and online, again

In [Offline query processing](/dsa/learn/rq-offline) you used the freedom of seeing every query first to answer them in a clever order. Here the distinction is about **decisions**:

- An **offline** algorithm sees the whole input, then decides. It can be optimal.
- An **online** algorithm receives the input piece by piece and must make an **irrevocable** decision about each piece before the next arrives.

Some problems are naturally online and lose nothing: a running sum, or reservoir sampling, which produces a perfect uniform sample without knowing the stream's length. Others pay a real price for not knowing the future. The classic example is about skis.

## Ski rental

You're on a ski trip of unknown length: you'll ski every day until the snow melts, and you don't know when that will be. Renting skis costs 1 a day; buying costs 10. Each morning you choose: rent again, or buy.

**With hindsight** it's easy: if the season lasts d days, the best cost is min(d, 10). Rent if it's short, buy on day 1 if it's long. Online, you don't know d. Compare three strategies:

```swift run
import Foundation

// Ski rental: renting costs 1 a day, buying costs 10. Three online strategies against hindsight.
let buyPrice = 10

/// What a strategy pays if the season lasts `days` days and it buys at the start of day `buyDay`
func cost(days: Int, buyDay: Int?) -> Int {
    guard let buyDay, buyDay <= days else { return days }    // never bought: rented every day
    return (buyDay - 1) + buyPrice                           // rented until then, then bought
}

func optimal(days: Int) -> Int { min(days, buyPrice) }      // with hindsight: the cheaper of the two

let strategies: [(name: String, buyDay: Int?)] = [("always rent", nil), ("buy at once", 1), ("break-even", buyPrice)]
func padded(_ text: String) -> String { String(repeating: " ", count: 13 - text.count) + text }
print("days  best" + strategies.map { padded($0.name) }.joined())
for days in [1, 5, 9, 10, 11, 30] {
    let costs = strategies.map { padded(String(cost(days: days, buyDay: $0.buyDay))) }
    print(String(format: "%4d  %4d", days, optimal(days: days)) + costs.joined())
}
for strategy in strategies {
    let worst = (1...1_000).map { Double(cost(days: $0, buyDay: strategy.buyDay)) / Double(optimal(days: $0)) }.max()!
    print(strategy.name + String(format: ": worst ratio over 1...1000 days = %.2f", worst))
}
```

```output
days  best  always rent  buy at once   break-even
   1     1            1           10            1
   5     5            5           10            5
   9     9            9           10            9
  10    10           10           10           19
  11    10           11           10           19
  30    10           30           10           19
always rent: worst ratio over 1...1000 days = 100.00
buy at once: worst ratio over 1...1000 days = 10.00
break-even: worst ratio over 1...1000 days = 1.90
```

- **Always rent** is perfect for short seasons and terrible for long ones: after 1,000 days it has paid 100 times the best cost, and the ratio grows without limit.
- **Buy at once** is perfect for long seasons and 10 times too expensive if the snow melts after one day.
- **Break-even**: rent for 9 days, and buy on day 10, the day your rentals would reach the buying price. If the season ends before day 10, you paid exactly the best cost. If it lasts longer, you paid 9 + 10 = 19 while hindsight paid 10. Its worst ratio is **19/10 = 1.9**, whatever the season does.

In general, with a buying price of B, break-even pays at most 2B − 1 against hindsight's B: a ratio **below 2**, always. And no deterministic strategy can do better. Whichever day you plan to buy on, an adversary who knows your plan ends the season that very day, so you pay your rentals plus the full price for one day of use. Buying on day B balances the two ways of losing.

## The competitive ratio

That idea has a name. An online algorithm is **c-competitive** if, on **every** input,

```text
cost of the online algorithm ≤ c × cost of the best offline solution (+ a constant)
```

Break-even ski rental is 2-competitive (precisely, 2 − 1/B). Always renting isn't c-competitive for any c. In plain words, the competitive ratio is **the price of not knowing the future**, measured against an opponent who picks the worst possible future for you.

Notice what it isn't: an average. It's a guarantee against the worst case, like O(n²) for quick sort with a fixed pivot. That makes it pessimistic, and sometimes misleading for typical inputs, but it's the only measure that doesn't need assumptions about what the future usually looks like.

### Randomness against an adversary

The adversary beat every deterministic plan by ending the season on your buying day. If you **choose your buying day at random** (from a carefully chosen distribution, favouring days near B), the adversary can no longer aim at it. The best randomised strategy has an expected ratio of e/(e − 1) ≈ **1.58**, better than any deterministic strategy's 2. It's the same trick as random pivots in quickselect: randomness denies the adversary a fixed target.

<Callout kind="analogy" title="Renting versus buying in real life">
The break-even rule is common sense with a proof attached. Keep renting until you've spent as much as buying would have cost, then buy. You'll never pay more than about twice what perfect hindsight would have paid, and you never needed to predict anything. The same rule decides when a phone should stop polling and open a push connection, when a database should build an index for a query it keeps seeing, or when to stop paying for cloud capacity by the hour and reserve it.
</Callout>

## Hiring on the spot: the secretary problem

A different online flavour. n candidates arrive one at a time **in random order**. After each interview you must hire or reject on the spot; rejected candidates are gone. You want to hire the **best** one. Picking at random succeeds 1 time in n.

The famous strategy: interview the first r candidates without hiring anyone, just to set a benchmark. Then hire the first candidate who beats everyone so far. Which r works best?

```swift run
import Foundation

// The secretary problem: candidates arrive in random order; hire on the spot or lose them forever.
// Strategy: watch the first r without hiring, then hire the first one better than all of those.
struct SeededGenerator: RandomNumberGenerator {
    private var state: UInt64
    init(seed: UInt64) { state = seed }
    mutating func next() -> UInt64 {          // SplitMix64
        state &+= 0x9E37_79B9_7F4A_7C15
        var z = state
        z = (z ^ (z >> 30)) &* 0xBF58_476D_1CE4_E5B9
        z = (z ^ (z >> 27)) &* 0x94D0_49BB_1331_11EB
        return z ^ (z >> 31)
    }
}

var generator = SeededGenerator(seed: 37)
let n = 100
let trials = 4_000
for r in [1, 10, 25, 37, 50, 75] {
    var hiredTheBest = 0
    for _ in 0..<trials {
        var scores = Array(0..<n)                         // n − 1 is the best candidate
        for i in stride(from: n - 1, through: 1, by: -1) { // Fisher–Yates
            scores.swapAt(i, Int(generator.next() % UInt64(i + 1)))
        }
        let benchmark = scores[0..<r].max()!
        if let hired = scores[r...].first(where: { $0 > benchmark }), hired == n - 1 {
            hiredTheBest += 1
        }
    }
    print(String(format: "watch %2d, then hire: best candidate %.3f of the time", r, Double(hiredTheBest) / Double(trials)))
}
print(String(format: "1/e = %.3f", 1 / exp(1.0)))
```

```output
watch  1, then hire: best candidate 0.049 of the time
watch 10, then hire: best candidate 0.240 of the time
watch 25, then hire: best candidate 0.349 of the time
watch 37, then hire: best candidate 0.378 of the time
watch 50, then hire: best candidate 0.356 of the time
watch 75, then hire: best candidate 0.219 of the time
1/e = 0.368
```

Watching about **37%** of the candidates first (n/e) hires the very best one about **37%** of the time (1/e), for any n: a hundred candidates or a million. Watch too few and the benchmark is weak, so you hire too early; watch too many and the best has often gone by. Here the randomness is in the **input order**, not the algorithm, and the Fisher–Yates loop from the first lesson of this module supplies it.

## Recognising online problems

Ask: **must I commit before I see the rest?** If yes, think online:

- **Caching and paging.** Which item to evict, with future requests unknown. LRU is k-competitive for a cache of k items, and no deterministic policy does better; the offline optimum evicts the item whose next use is furthest in the future. Next lesson.
- **Load balancing.** Send each job to the least-loaded server as it arrives: about 2-competitive against the best assignment in hindsight.
- **Interval scheduling as events arrive**, matching riders to drivers, admitting requests into a network.

If you **can** wait and see everything, an offline algorithm (sorting, DP, the sweeps of M62) is usually simpler and optimal.

<Callout kind="gotcha" title="Common mistakes">
- **Comparing against the wrong yardstick.** The competitive ratio compares with the best **offline** solution for the same input, not with another online algorithm.
- **Treating the ratio as an average.** It's a worst-case guarantee. On typical inputs an online algorithm may be much closer to optimal.
- **Forgetting irrevocability.** If you can undo a decision cheaply later, the problem may not be online at all.
- **Secretary strategy with a known best value.** The 37% rule is for when you can only compare candidates. If you know the scores' distribution, better strategies exist.
</Callout>
