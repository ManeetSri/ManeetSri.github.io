## Why it matters

In six months you won't remember why you chose a file cache over Core Data, only that you did. Design is a chain of decisions, and the reasons are what you need again: to change your mind well, to explain a design to a colleague, and to answer "tell me about a decision you made" in an interview. A design journal keeps the reasons. It takes five minutes a session.

## What to record

Not every decision needs an entry. Record the one-way doors from [Architecture, design and implementation decisions](/design/learn/sd-orientation-decisions), and any choice you went back and forth on. For each:

- **The decision**, in one sentence.
- **The context:** the requirements, numbers and constraints pushing on it.
- **The alternatives** you considered, including doing nothing.
- **Why this one**, and what you gave up for it.
- **When to revisit it:** the signal that would make you decide differently.

Record what practice teaches you, too: what the model answer covered that yours didn't, the finding you missed in a review, the mock criterion you scored lowest. The console's mistakes log tells you *that* you missed something; the journal says *what*, and *why* you missed it.

## A lightweight ADR

An **architecture decision record** (ADR) is a short document about one decision. Michael Nygard's original format, from 2011, has five parts: a title, a status, the context, the decision and its consequences. Adding the alternatives you rejected makes it far more useful later. Here's one for the forecast screen exercise:

```text title="decisions/0003-cache-the-forecast-in-a-file.md"
# 3. Cache the forecast in a Codable file

Status: Accepted

Context
The forecast screen must open instantly and work offline. We show one saved
place. A forecast is about 15 KB of JSON and is replaced whole on each refresh.

Decision
Store the last forecast, its ETag and when it was fetched as one Codable file
in the Caches directory. Show it first, then refresh in the background.

Alternatives
- Core Data: queries we don't need, and a schema we'd have to migrate.
- No cache: a spinner on every launch, and nothing at all offline.

Consequences
+ Instant open and offline support in very little code.
- iOS may clear Caches when storage runs low; then we fetch again.
- Several places with search would need a real database. Revisit then.
```

That's the whole thing: a few minutes to write, and it answers every question a colleague, or you in a year, would ask about the choice. You'll write fuller ADRs in D09 and D31. For a journal, this skeleton is plenty.

## Where to keep it

| Where | Next to the code | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The console's notes boxes, gathered on the Notes page | No | Reflections on lessons and exercises |
| A `decisions/` folder of Markdown files in your app's repository | Yes | Real decisions on real projects, shared with the team |
| Any notes app you already use | No | Whatever you'll actually open |

The best place is the one you'll open every week.

## Reviewing it weekly

Fifteen minutes, at the same time each week:

1. **Reread the week's entries.** Would you decide the same way now? If not, write down what changed.
2. **Look for repeats** in the mistakes log on the Notes page. The same rubric skill scored low twice is a pattern, not bad luck.
3. **Pick one thing to practise** next week: an exercise to start over, a lesson to reread, a review to try again.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing what, not why.** "Used a file cache" is useless in a year; the reasons are the point.
- **Writing it later.** A week on, you remember the choice and forget the alternatives. Write while it's fresh.
- **Never rereading it.** A journal you don't review is a diary. The weekly review is where it earns its keep.

<Callout kind="interview">

Design and behavioural rounds both ask some version of: "Tell me about a technical decision you made. What else did you consider? Would you make it again?" A journal is a bank of true, specific answers, with the alternatives and costs already worked out. **Red flags:** a decision with no alternatives, and no idea what it cost.

</Callout>
