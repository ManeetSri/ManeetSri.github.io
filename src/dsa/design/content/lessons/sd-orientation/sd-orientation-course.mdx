## Why it matters

This course is long: 76 modules, more than 700 lessons and roughly 275 hours of reading before you count the practice. System design also has no single correct order and few right answers, so it's easy to drift between topics without getting better at designing. Fifteen minutes on how the course is built means every later session goes on the thing that moves you forward.

## What you're learning

Two words run through everything here, and they overlap.

- **Software architecture** is how an app is divided into parts, what each part is responsible for, and which parts depend on which. "The screen talks to a view model, which talks to a repository, which owns the cache" is an architecture.
- **System design** zooms out to the whole product: the app, the API it calls, the servers and storage behind that API, and how all of it behaves when networks fail, users multiply and the team grows.

On iOS the two meet constantly. Whether a feed works on a train is decided partly by the server (can it send changes since a cursor?) and partly by the app (does it keep a local copy and show it first?). You'll learn both sides, leaning towards the side you ship: the app.

## The roadmap

The course has **12 parts**. Each builds on the ones before it, and each module lists the modules it needs.

| Part | Title | What you'll be able to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Start here | Study well, and follow one framework for every design |
| I | Software design foundations | Judge designs by quality attributes, coupling and cohesion, and use SOLID and Swift's own tools |
| II | Design patterns | Recognise and apply the classic patterns, Apple's own, and the resilience patterns |
| III | iOS platform architecture | Design around the app lifecycle, UIKit, SwiftUI, state, navigation, DI and concurrency |
| IV | App architecture patterns | Choose between MVC, MVVM, TCA, VIPER and Clean, and migrate between them |
| V | The data layer | Design networking, APIs, persistence, caching, offline sync and media pipelines |
| VI | Backend for mobile engineers | Estimate load and sketch the server side an app needs |
| VII | Cross-cutting concerns | Build in security, privacy, performance, background work, flags, analytics and payments |
| VIII | Testing & delivery | Design for testability, and ship safely |
| IX | Scaling apps & teams | Modularise, build design systems and SDKs, and work across platforms |
| X | Interviews & case studies | Run a mobile system design interview, and design dozens of real apps |
| XI | Leadership & capstones | Lead architecture decisions, and build capstone projects |

Every lesson has the same shape: why it matters, the idea in small steps with Swift and diagrams you can step through, a **trade-offs** table, what to say **in the interview**, the iOS gotchas, then takeaways and a quiz. Every module ends with a **checkpoint**: 12 questions from the whole module, and 80% marks it mastered.

## Four kinds of practice

Reading about design doesn't make you better at it; designing does. Practice items appear under the lessons they belong to, and all of them are on the **Practice** page.

| Kind | What you do | How it's judged |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Design exercise** | Read a brief with its priorities, numbers and constraints, then write your design in a structured workspace, one box per part. It autosaves. | Reveal the model answer beside yours, then score each rubric criterion from "Not yet" to "Strong". |
| **Architecture review** | Read realistic code or a written design and review it like a pull request: problems, a plan to fix them, tests to add. | Tick each expected finding as found, partly found or missed, weighted by severity. |
| **Swift kata** | Build a real component, such as a retry policy, a cache or a coordinator, starting from a stub in the companion package. | Its tests pass or fail: `swift test --filter <Kata>Tests`. |
| **Mock design interview** | 45 or 60 minutes on a random design exercise from topics you've finished, with a timer and no model answer. | Score yourself 1 to 4 on the eight things interviewers judge. |

Your weakest areas collect in the **mistakes log** on the Notes page: questions you missed first time, rubric skills you scored low, findings you missed and mocks that went badly. **Progress** shows your latest score for each design skill, so you can see whether you're weaker on, say, offline handling than on API design.

## Review and spaced repetition

When you mark a lesson complete, its takeaway cards join your **Review** queue. A quiz question you miss on the first try becomes a card too. Design exercises and reviews come back as well: you outline the design from memory, then compare it with what a strong answer covers.

Cards return on a growing schedule, just before you'd forget them. Rate honestly (Again, Hard, Good or Easy), because the rating sets the next date. **Today** plans each session around your daily minutes: reviews first, then the next lesson, then practice. Start every session there.

## Two courses, one console

The brand in the top bar is a **course switcher**: it opens a menu with DSA in Swift and System Design, each showing how far along your path you are. On a phone it's in the modules sheet. The two courses share your **streak and XP**, so any study day counts. Everything else belongs to one course: its goal and pace, progress, review queue, notes and mocks. Press `⌘K` (or `/`) to search this course's lessons, practice and cheat sheets.

## How to study design

- **Write before you reveal.** The console asks you to confirm if you reveal a model answer after only a few words. Comparing a real attempt with a strong one is where you learn; reading the answer first only feels like learning.
- **Keep a design journal.** Write down each decision you make in an exercise, the alternatives, and why. [Keeping a design journal](/design/learn/sd-orientation-journal) shows how.
- **Say it out loud.** Interviews are spoken. Explain your design to an empty room while you write it.
- **Mix it up** once you've finished a few modules: a mock draws from everything you've learned, as real interviews do.

<Callout kind="interview">

Mock design interviews copy the real format: one open-ended prompt, 45 or 60 minutes, and you drive the conversation. Interviewers aren't checking whether you reach one particular design. They score how you clarify the problem, structure the answer, go deep on the hard part and weigh trade-offs, and your mocks use the same criteria. You'll study the interview itself in D66.

</Callout>
