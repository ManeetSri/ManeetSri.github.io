## Why it matters

A design question is open-ended on purpose. "Design a chat app" could fill a month, and you have 45 minutes. Without a structure, you spend twenty of them on whatever you know best and run out of time before the parts that matter. A framework gives every design the same backbone: you cover what matters, in a sensible order, and you always know your next step. Every design exercise, mock interview and case study in this course follows the one in this lesson.

## The framework at a glance

| Step | The question it answers | What you produce |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Understand | What are we building, for whom, and why? | A one-sentence summary and the main user journey |
| 2. Requirements | What must it do, and how well? | Functional and non-functional requirements, and what's out of scope |
| 3. Constraints & scale | Which numbers and limits shape it? | Estimates of users, requests and sizes; platform, team and existing systems |
| 4. High-level design | What are the big parts, and how does data flow? | Boxes and arrows across device and server |
| 5. API & data | What crosses the network, and what's stored where? | Endpoints and payloads; entities and where each lives |
| 6. Client architecture | How is the app put together? | Layers, state, navigation, dependencies, concurrency |
| 7. Deep dives | What's hardest, in detail? | Two or three parts worked through properly |
| 8. Failure & offline | What goes wrong, and what does the user see? | Behaviour on bad networks, errors, conflicts; security and privacy |
| 9. Trade-offs | What did you choose over what, and why? | Named alternatives and what they would have cost |
| 10. Evolution | How does it change as it grows? | What changes at ten times the users, with more features, with a bigger team |

## What each step is for

### 1. Understand

Restate the problem in your own words and walk through the main journey as a user would. It takes a minute and catches misunderstandings while they're still cheap: "design a photo app" might mean the camera, the gallery or the social feed.

### 2. Requirements

**Functional requirements** say what the system does: "people can like a post". **Non-functional requirements** say how well it does it: how fast, how fresh, how reliable, how private, how accessible, how kind to the battery. On mobile, the non-functional ones usually decide the design. Then agree what's **out of scope**, so you don't spend time on it and nobody marks you down for skipping it.

### 3. Constraints and scale

Numbers turn "it depends" into decisions. How many users, how many requests, how big is each payload, how much data per device? Add the constraints: iOS only or also Android, the oldest iOS version, an existing backend you must use, a team of three, a launch date. You'll practise estimating in [Reading a design prompt](/design/learn/sd-orientation-prompts).

### 4. High-level design

Draw the main parts on the device and on the server, and the path data takes between them. Stay at the level of boxes: screen, view model, repository, cache, API, database, push service. Details come later.

### 5. API and data

Define what crosses the network: the endpoints or messages, their request and response shapes, pagination and errors. Then the data: the entities, and where each one lives (memory, disk, server) and for how long.

### 6. Client architecture

Show how the app is put together: the layers, who owns which state, how screens get their dependencies, how navigation works, and which work runs where (main actor, background tasks). This is where a mobile interview spends much of its time.

### 7. Deep dives

Pick the two or three hardest parts and work through them properly: the cache's invalidation, the sync's conflicts, the image pipeline's memory. Breadth earned you the right to go deep; depth is where strong candidates stand apart.

### 8. Failure and offline

Walk through what goes wrong: no network, a slow network, a server error, an expired session, the app suspended mid-request, two devices editing the same thing. Say what the user sees in each case. Include security and privacy: what's sensitive, where it's stored, who can call what.

### 9. Trade-offs

Name the alternatives you rejected and why. "I chose X" is a statement; "I chose X over Y because Z matters more here, at the cost of W" is a design.

### 10. Evolution

Say how the design changes as it grows: ten times the users, the next three features, a second team. It shows you designed for today without blocking tomorrow.

<Callout kind="tip">

The design exercises give most steps their own box. Understanding goes into the requirements box and evolution into the trade-offs box, and the workspace puts client architecture straight after the high-level design. The order is a sensible default, not a rule.

</Callout>

## A worked example: a like button

Here's the framework applied to something small, so you can see every step at once. In a real exercise you'd write more; in an interview you'd say it.

> Add a like button to every post in a photo-sharing app's feed, and on each post's own screen.

### 1. Understand

People scroll the feed, tap the heart on a post they enjoy, and see it fill and the count go up. Tapping again unlikes it. The author sees how many people liked their post.

### 2. Requirements

- **Functional:** like and unlike a post; show whether I've liked it and how many likes it has, in the feed and on the post's screen.
- **Non-functional:** the heart responds within a frame or two of the tap, with no spinner; a like made offline isn't lost; repeated taps never double-count; the feed and the post's screen always agree.
- **Out of scope:** the list of who liked a post, notifying the author, and reactions other than a like.

### 3. Constraints and scale

- 5 million daily users who like about 10 posts each: 50 million likes a day. Divided by 86,400 seconds, that's about 580 a second, perhaps 1,700 at the evening peak.
- Each request is tiny, well under 1 KB. Counts arrive with the posts the feed already loads, so likes add no extra reads.
- One celebrity post can gather thousands of likes a minute, so its count is approximate anyway: "12.4k".

The numbers say something useful: 580 small writes a second is modest for a backend. The hard part of this feature is on the phone.

### 4. High-level design

Step through it. Watch where the heart fills: before the network is involved at all.

### 5. API and data

```http
PUT /posts/42/like       → 200 {"liked": true, "likeCount": 1207}
DELETE /posts/42/like    → 200 {"liked": false, "likeCount": 1206}
```

`PUT` and `DELETE` *set* the state instead of flipping it, so they're **idempotent**: sending the same request twice has the same effect as sending it once. That makes retries safe. A `POST /posts/42/toggle-like` repeated by a retry would like the post and then unlike it.

Each post carries `likeCount` and `likedByMe`. On the device, the post store holds them for every loaded post, and the outbox holds at most one pending intent per post: the post's id and whether it should end up liked.

### 6. Client architecture

- The feed's and the post screen's view models both read posts from one **post store**. Neither keeps its own copy.
- A **like service** owns the whole flow: the optimistic update, the request, the reconciliation and the rollback.
- The API client and the outbox are **injected**, so tests replace them with stubs.

The heart of the like service is a few lines:

```swift check title="LikeService.swift"
import Foundation

struct LikeState: Equatable, Sendable {
    var isLiked: Bool
    var count: Int
}

protocol LikesAPI: Sendable {
    /// `PUT` or `DELETE /posts/{id}/like`. Returns the server's count.
    func setLiked(_ liked: Bool, postID: String) async throws -> Int
}

/// The one place a post's like state lives, so every screen shows the same heart
@MainActor
final class LikeService {
    private(set) var states: [String: LikeState] = [:]
    private let api: any LikesAPI

    init(api: any LikesAPI) {
        self.api = api
    }

    func toggleLike(on postID: String) async {
        guard let before = states[postID] else { return }
        let liked = !before.isLiked
        // Optimistic: show the change now, before the server has said yes
        states[postID] = LikeState(isLiked: liked, count: before.count + (liked ? 1 : -1))
        do {
            let count = try await api.setLiked(liked, postID: postID)
            states[postID]?.count = count
        } catch {
            // Roll back. (A second tap during the await is a deep dive of its own.)
            states[postID] = before
        }
    }
}
```

### 7. Deep dives

**Optimistic update and rollback.** The screen changes before the server answers, so the design needs a plan for when the server says no:

```visual sequence
title: "Deep dive: an optimistic like, and its rollback"
participants:
  - { id: feed, label: Feed screen }
  - { id: likes, label: Like service }
  - { id: store, label: Post store }
  - { id: api, label: Likes API }
messages:
  - [feed, likes, toggleLike]
  - [likes, store, "liked, 1,204", { say: "The service updates the store **before** asking the server, remembering the old state in case it must undo." }]
  - [store, feed, redraw, { async: true, say: "The heart fills straight away. Nothing has touched the network yet." }]
  - [likes, api, "PUT /posts/42/like", { say: "Now the request goes out, in the background of the user's experience." }]
  - [api, likes, "200, count 1,207", { reply: true, say: "Usually the server agrees. Its count, which includes other people's likes meanwhile, replaces the guess." }]
  - [likes, store, "count 1,207"]
  - [api, likes, "404, post deleted", { reply: true, say: "Sometimes it doesn't: the post was deleted, or its author blocked you." }]
  - [likes, store, roll back, { say: "The service restores the state it saved before the tap." }]
  - [store, feed, redraw + message, { async: true, say: "The heart empties, and a short message says why. Silently undoing a tap would feel like a bug." }]
fragments:
  - { kind: alt, label: success, from: 4, to: 8, else: 6, elseLabel: rejected }
```

**Rapid taps.** Someone taps like, unlike, like within a second. Three requests are wasteful and can arrive in any order, so the server might end with the wrong one. Instead, keep only the **latest intent** per post and send it once the request in flight has finished: the server sees the final state, and the rollback restores the last state the server confirmed, not whatever was on screen before the most recent tap.

**A fresh page arrives mid-like.** If the feed reloads while a like is pending, the new page carries the server's old count. The pending intent wins until the server confirms it, or the heart flickers.

### 8. Failure and offline

- **Offline or a timeout:** keep the heart filled, write the intent to the outbox, and retry with backoff when the connection returns.
- **Post deleted (404) or not allowed (403):** roll back and say why.
- **Session expired (401):** refresh the token, then retry once.
- **App suspended before the request goes out:** the outbox is on disk, so the next launch sends it.
- **Security:** the server checks the user may see the post, and the app sends only an intent, never a count.

### 9. Trade-offs

| Approach | Feels instant | Never shows a wrong state | Survives offline | Complexity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wait for the server, with a spinner | No | Yes | No | Low |
| Optimistic, roll back on failure | Yes | No: briefly wrong after a failure | No | Medium |
| Optimistic, plus a persisted outbox | Yes | No: briefly wrong after a rejection | Yes | Higher |

For a like, being wrong for a moment costs nothing and waiting for every tap costs a lot, so optimistic with an outbox wins. For a payment, the first row wins: you never show "paid" before the server confirms it.

### 10. Evolution

- **More reactions:** `PUT /posts/42/reaction` with a type; the outbox still keeps one intent per post.
- **Viral posts:** the backend counts in shards and approximates; the app already shows rounded numbers.
- **Live counts on an open post:** refresh it only while it's on screen.
- **Back online after a long trip:** send the outbox's intents in one batched request.

## Fitting it into 45 minutes

| Step | Roughly |
| --- | --- |
| Understand and requirements | 6 minutes |
| Constraints and scale | 4 minutes |
| High-level design | 7 minutes |
| API and data | 7 minutes |
| Client architecture | 8 minutes |
| Deep dives | 8 minutes |
| Failure, trade-offs and evolution | 5 minutes, plus whatever you've woven in earlier |

The framework isn't a waterfall. A deep dive often reveals a missing requirement, and you go back and add it. Interviewers also steer: if they ask about the cache in minute ten, follow them, then return to your map.

<Callout kind="interview">

**Say** your plan in the first minute: "I'll clarify requirements, do some quick numbers, sketch the big picture, then go deep on the client." Then check in at each step: "Does this level of detail work, or shall I go deeper here?" **Ask** which part interests them most when you choose your deep dives. **Red flags:** drawing classes before agreeing requirements, no numbers at all, never mentioning failure, and running out of time before naming a single trade-off.

</Callout>

<Callout kind="gotcha" title="iOS gotcha: a tap isn't a request">

If someone likes a post and immediately swipes home, the app can be suspended before the request leaves, and terminated before it resumes. An intent that lives only in memory is lost without a trace. Write it to the outbox first (a small file or database row is enough), then send it. Whatever's left in the outbox at the next launch gets sent then.

</Callout>

## Practice

[A weather app's forecast screen](/design/practice/sd-design-forecast-screen) is your first full design: a screen that must open instantly and still show something sensible on a train. It skips the estimates box, so you can focus on the rest of the framework. Give it 30 minutes, write in every box before you reveal the model answer, then score yourself against the rubric honestly. Where the model answer covers something you didn't, write it in your design journal.

```cards
- id: framework-steps
  front: "What are the ten steps of the design framework?"
  back: "Understand → requirements → constraints & scale → high-level design → API & data → client architecture → deep dives → failure & offline → trade-offs → evolution."
- id: functional-vs-non-functional
  front: "What's the difference between functional and non-functional requirements?"
  back: "Functional ones say what the system does (like a post). Non-functional ones say how well: speed, freshness, reliability, privacy, battery. On mobile, the non-functional ones usually shape the design."
- id: idempotent-like
  front: "Why does the like API use `PUT` and `DELETE` instead of a toggle?"
  back: "They set the final state, so they're idempotent: a retry or a duplicate has the same effect as one request. A toggle sent twice undoes itself."
- id: optimistic-update
  front: "What is an optimistic update, and what does it need?"
  back: "Showing a change before the server confirms it, so the app feels instant. It needs the previous state kept for a rollback, and a way to tell the user when the server says no."
```
