## Why it matters

"Online", "last seen 5 minutes ago" and "Ana is typing…" look like the smallest features in a chat app. They're among the most expensive. Presence changes constantly and has to reach everyone who can see it, so it can generate more traffic than the messages themselves, and typing indicators are a stream of tiny events that must be throttled on one side and expired on the other. Interviewers use them to check whether you know which data deserves guarantees and which should be cheap, lossy and short-lived.

## Presence is derived, not stored

Nobody tells the server "I'm online". The server **infers** it from connections: a user is online while at least one of their devices holds a live connection whose heartbeats are arriving. That makes presence a by-product of the reconnection machinery you built in the last lessons, with three states most apps show:

- **Online**: a device is connected and in the foreground.
- **Away** (or idle): connected but backgrounded, or no interaction for some minutes. Apps that close their socket in the background, as they should on iOS, usually skip this state and go straight to offline.
- **Offline**, with **last seen**: the time the last connection went away.

A store with **expiring keys** (Redis is the usual choice) suits this exactly: each connection writes a key with a TTL, heartbeats refresh it, and a crashed gateway's entries simply expire. Nothing needs to remember to clean up.

### Flapping and the grace period

A phone in a lift drops for ten seconds and comes back. Publishing "offline" and then "online" ten seconds later is noise for everyone watching and costs a fan-out each way. A **grace period** of 30–60 seconds before announcing offline absorbs nearly all of these blips; online can be announced at once. The same idea applies on the device: show "online" from the last state you received, and don't animate every flicker.

### Why presence is expensive: fan-out

Every presence change goes to everyone who can see it. Suppose 10 million users are online at peak, each can see about 200 contacts, and each changes state about 10 times a day (opening and leaving the app). That's 10M × 10 × 200 = 20 billion presence deliveries a day, more than 200,000 a second on average, almost all to people who aren't looking. Delivered naively, presence outweighs chat.

The fixes all come down to **sending presence only where it's seen**:

- **Subscribe to what's on screen.** The app subscribes to the presence of the people in the visible conversation list or the open chat, and unsubscribes when they scroll away. Slack moved its clients to this kind of presence subscription as workspaces grew; before that, every change went to every connected member.
- **Fetch lazily.** "Last seen" is fetched when someone opens a profile, not pushed to everyone on every change.
- **Batch and coarsen.** Send presence changes in batches every few seconds, and prefer coarse states ("active today") where precision doesn't matter.
- **Give up for large groups.** In a 5,000-member channel, show "312 online" from a periodic count instead of per-person dots.

### Presence is personal data

Online status and last-seen times reveal when someone sleeps, works and talks to whom. Treat them like any other personal data:

- Let users hide them. WhatsApp, for example, lets people choose who sees their last seen and online status, and not show theirs to anyone.
- Never send presence to people the user has blocked, and enforce visibility on the server, not by hiding it in the app.
- Don't keep presence history you don't need. A current state with a TTL is enough for the feature.

## Typing indicators

A typing indicator is the purest **at-most-once** data there is: it's useful for a few seconds, worthless afterwards, and harmless if lost. So it should cost almost nothing:

- never stored on the server, never queued for offline users, never sent through APNs;
- sent only to members of the conversation who are connected right now;
- throttled on the sender, and expired on the receiver.

### The sender: throttle and stop

Sending an event per keystroke would be five or more messages a second per typist. Instead:

1. On the **first keystroke**, send `typing` at once (the leading edge), so the indicator appears immediately.
2. While typing continues, send `typing` again at most every **3 seconds**. That's a throttle, as you met in [debounce, throttle and coalesce](/design/learn/sd-resilience-debounce-throttle), and its job here is to refresh the receiver before its indicator expires.
3. Send `stopped` when the field is **cleared**, or after **5 seconds** with no keystrokes.
4. When the message is **sent**, send nothing: the message itself tells everyone to clear the indicator.

### The receiver: expire and ignore yourself

The receiver must survive a `stopped` that never comes (the sender's app was suspended, the signal was dropped). Each `typing` signal is therefore valid only for a limited time, the **expiry**, and a new one extends it. Choose the expiry longer than two refresh intervals plus some latency (8 seconds against a 3-second refresh), so one lost refresh doesn't make the indicator flicker off mid-sentence. Clear a person at once on `stopped` or when their message arrives, and ignore your own signals, which the server may echo back.

In a group, the receiver keeps one expiry per person and summarises: "Ana is typing…", "Ana and Ben are typing…", then "Several people are typing…". For very large groups, the server can stop relaying typing altogether; a dozen strangers typing tells nobody anything.

### Wiring it into SwiftUI

The sending rules live in a small object the composer calls on every change; the receiving side needs no timer per person, because `TimelineView` re-evaluates the expiries every second:

```swift check ios title="Typing.swift"
import SwiftUI

/// Turns keystrokes into a few typing signals: one at once, a refresh at most every 3 s while typing goes
/// on, and "stopped" when the field is cleared or after 5 quiet seconds
@MainActor
final class TypingSignals {
    private let send: @MainActor (_ isTyping: Bool) -> Void
    private let clock = ContinuousClock()
    private var lastSent: ContinuousClock.Instant?
    private var quietTimer: Task<Void, Never>?

    init(send: @escaping @MainActor (_ isTyping: Bool) -> Void) {
        self.send = send
    }

    func textChanged(_ text: String) {
        quietTimer?.cancel()
        guard !text.isEmpty else {
            stop()
            return
        }
        let now = clock.now
        if lastSent.map({ now - $0 >= .seconds(3) }) ?? true {
            lastSent = now
            send(true)
        }
        quietTimer = Task { [weak self] in
            try? await Task.sleep(for: .seconds(5))
            if !Task.isCancelled { self?.stop() }
        }
    }

    /// The message itself clears the indicator for everyone else, so no signal is sent
    func messageSent() {
        quietTimer?.cancel()
        lastSent = nil
    }

    private func stop() {
        guard lastSent != nil else { return }
        lastSent = nil
        send(false)
    }
}

struct ComposerView: View {
    @State private var draft = ""
    let typing: TypingSignals
    let sendMessage: (String) -> Void

    var body: some View {
        HStack {
            TextField("Message", text: $draft, axis: .vertical)
                .onChange(of: draft) { _, text in typing.textChanged(text) }
            Button("Send") {
                sendMessage(draft)
                typing.messageSent()
                draft = ""
            }
            .disabled(draft.isEmpty)
        }
    }
}

/// Shows who's typing. Each name stays until its last signal expires; TimelineView re-checks every second,
/// so expiry needs no timer per person
struct TypingBanner: View {
    let typingUntil: [String: Date]

    var body: some View {
        TimelineView(.periodic(from: .now, by: 1)) { context in
            let names = typingUntil.filter { $0.value > context.date }.keys.sorted()
            if let summary = Self.summary(of: names) {
                Text(summary).font(.footnote).foregroundStyle(.secondary)
            }
        }
    }

    static func summary(of names: [String]) -> String? {
        switch names.count {
        case 0: nil
        case 1: "\(names[0]) is typing…"
        case 2: "\(names[0]) and \(names[1]) are typing…"
        default: "Several people are typing…"
        }
    }
}
```

`messageSent()` runs before the draft is cleared, so the empty draft that follows finds nothing announced and sends nothing. In production, pass a clock in instead of creating `ContinuousClock` inside, so the rules can be tested without waiting, which is what the kata for this lesson does.

## Trade-offs

| Decision | Cheap option | Rich option | Choose rich when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who receives presence | Only subscribers to what's on screen | Every contact, all the time | Contact lists are small (a family app) |
| Offline detection | Heartbeat TTL plus a 30–60 s grace period | Immediate, on every disconnect | Almost never: flapping costs more than it tells |
| Presence precision | "Active today", or a count in big groups | A live dot and exact last seen | One-to-one and small-group chat |
| Typing frequency | A refresh every 3–5 s, expiry about 8 s | An event per keystroke | Never: it's the same indicator for many times the traffic |
| Typing in large groups | Off, or "several people" | Every name | Groups up to a dozen or so |

<Callout kind="interview">

**Say** that both are ephemeral and say what that buys: "Presence is derived from live connections with a TTL in Redis, aggregated per user across devices, with a grace period before offline. It's only pushed to clients that subscribe to the people on screen, and last seen is fetched on demand. Typing signals are at most once: throttled to one every 3 seconds on the sender, expiring after 8 on the receiver, never stored or pushed." **Ask** about group sizes and privacy settings. **Red flags:** storing typing events, pushing presence through APNs, broadcasting every presence change to every contact, and an indicator that relies on `stopped` always arriving.

</Callout>

<Callout kind="gotcha" title="iOS gotcha: 'online' while suspended">

A suspended app can't send heartbeats, but the server may still list the user as online until its TTL and grace period run out, sometimes a minute or more. If "online" matters to your product (a support agent's availability, a dating app), have the app send an explicit `away` or close its socket cleanly when the scene enters the background, which takes a moment and gives the server an immediate, accurate signal.

</Callout>

The kata **"A typing indicator"** builds both sides as plain value types driven by timestamps: a sender that throttles and stops, and a roster that expires stale signals, ignores your own echoes and writes the summary line.

```cards
  front: "Why wait 30–60 seconds before announcing that someone went offline?"
  back: "Phones drop for seconds at a time in lifts and handovers. A grace period absorbs the flaps, which would otherwise cost an offline and an online fan-out each."
- id: presence-fanout
  front: "How do you keep presence fan-out affordable?"
  back: "Send it only where it's seen: clients subscribe to the presence of people on screen, last seen is fetched on demand, changes are batched, and large groups get a count."
- id: typing-rules
  front: "What are the sender and receiver rules for a typing indicator?"
  back: "Sender: send at once, refresh at most every 3 s, stop on clear or 5 s idle, and let the sent message clear it. Receiver: expire each signal after about 8 s and ignore your own."
```
