## Why it matters

A live connection that costs nothing on a server can cost a phone a noticeable slice of its battery, and users find out from the Battery screen in Settings, which names your app. The cost isn't the bytes; it's how often you wake the cellular radio and how long it stays awake afterwards. Every choice in this module (polling interval, heartbeat interval, coalescing, when to close) is also a battery choice, and interviewers for senior roles expect you to make it out loud.

## The radio's tail

You met the cellular radio's power states in [radio states and cellular links](/design/learn/sd-net-radio-cellular). The short version that matters here:

- An idle radio uses very little power, but sending anything first needs a **promotion** to the connected state, which takes from tens to hundreds of milliseconds and some signalling with the network.
- After the last packet, the radio doesn't drop back at once. It stays in a high-power state for a **tail** of several seconds, set by the carrier's network, in case more data follows. Measurements of LTE networks have found tails of around ten seconds.
- 5G adds an inactive state that resumes faster, and Wi-Fi sleeps between packets far more cheaply, but the principle holds everywhere: **each wake-up costs a fixed chunk of energy, almost regardless of how much you send.**

So a 60-byte heartbeat costs about the same radio time as a 60 KB message: the promotion plus the tail. Ten small transmissions spread over a minute keep the radio awake for most of that minute; the same ten bundled into one burst keep it awake for one tail.

With a 30-second heartbeat and a 10-second tail, the radio is awake a third of the time the connection is open: about 2% of a typical battery per hour, from traffic that carries no information. Drop the interval to 10 seconds and it never sleeps. Stretch it to 2 minutes and the cost falls by three quarters, but now the 60-second idle timeouts on the path close the connection for you. The real numbers vary with the network, the signal and the device (a weak signal costs much more), so treat the outputs as orders of magnitude, not a forecast.

## What iOS does for you

The platform already protects the battery in ways that shape every real-time design:

- **Suspension.** A few seconds after the user leaves, your app stops running, and its connections stop with it. That's the single biggest reason real-time apps don't drain phones overnight, and why the background mode belongs to push.
- **One push connection for every app.** The system keeps a single long-lived connection to APNs, with its keep-alive tuned by Apple for each network, shared by every app on the device. A push costs your app no idle connection at all. A dozen apps each holding their own socket in the background would cost a dozen sets of heartbeats.
- **Low Power Mode.** When the user turns it on (or iOS suggests it at 20%), the system cuts back background activity such as background app refresh. `ProcessInfo.processInfo.isLowPowerModeEnabled` tells you, and the `NSProcessInfoPowerStateDidChange` notification says when it changes. Your app should cut back too.
- **Low Data Mode and expensive networks.** `NWPath.isConstrained` is true in Low Data Mode, and `isExpensive` on cellular and personal hotspots. Setting `allowsConstrainedNetworkAccess = false` on a `URLSessionConfiguration` for non-essential traffic (prefetching, analytics) makes those requests fail fast in Low Data Mode instead of using the user's data.
- **Discretionary transfers.** A background `URLSession` with `isDiscretionary = true` lets the system schedule big transfers for when the device is on Wi-Fi and charging.

## Designing for the radio

Most of the savings come from a handful of habits:

- **One connection per app**, shared by every screen. Two sockets mean two sets of heartbeats that rarely line up.
- **Heartbeat only when quiet.** Any frame received proves the connection is alive; reset the ping timer on traffic so a busy connection never sends a heartbeat at all.
- **Batch what you send.** Read receipts, typing stops, analytics and "message delivered" acks can wait a second or two and go together, in one wake-up instead of five. Piggyback on wake-ups you're making anyway: if you're about to send a message, flush the pending receipts with it.
- **Coalesce what the server sends.** For data that changes many times a second (match statistics, prices, a moving map), the server can send the latest state once a second instead of every change. The user sees the same screen; the radio sees a fraction of the wake-ups.
- **Close when nothing live is on screen.** Unsubscribe when a live screen disappears, and close the socket altogether once nothing has needed it for a while (say a minute), rather than keeping it open "in case".
- **Adapt to the user's settings.** In Low Power Mode or Low Data Mode, stretch the heartbeat (staying under the path's idle timeouts), coalesce more, and stop prefetching.
- **Let push do the background.** Never poll or keep sockets alive in the background to save a few seconds of latency; send a push and sync on open.

The adaptation is a few lines. Pick a policy from the two system signals, and hand it to the connection:

```swift check ios title="LivenessMonitor.swift"
import Foundation
import Network

/// How lively the connection should be: frequent heartbeats and every update only when the user can afford them
struct LivenessPolicy: Equatable, Sendable {
    var heartbeat: Duration
    var coalesceUpdates: Duration

    static let normal = LivenessPolicy(heartbeat: .seconds(25), coalesceUpdates: .zero)
    // Still under the 60-second idle timeouts common on proxies and load balancers
    static let frugal = LivenessPolicy(heartbeat: .seconds(45), coalesceUpdates: .seconds(2))
}

/// Picks the policy from Low Power Mode and Low Data Mode, and updates it when either changes
@MainActor @Observable
final class LivenessMonitor {
    private(set) var policy = LivenessPolicy.normal
    private var lowPower = ProcessInfo.processInfo.isLowPowerModeEnabled
    private var lowData = false

    /// Runs for as long as the calling task does
    func run() async {
        // A path monitor can't be restarted once cancelled, so each run gets its own
        let monitor = NWPathMonitor()
        monitor.pathUpdateHandler = { [weak self] path in
            let constrained = path.isConstrained
            Task { @MainActor in self?.lowDataChanged(constrained) }
        }
        monitor.start(queue: DispatchQueue(label: "liveness.path"))
        defer { monitor.cancel() }
        choose()
        for await _ in NotificationCenter.default.notifications(named: .NSProcessInfoPowerStateDidChange) {
            lowPower = ProcessInfo.processInfo.isLowPowerModeEnabled
            choose()
        }
    }

    private func lowDataChanged(_ constrained: Bool) {
        lowData = constrained
        choose()
    }

    private func choose() {
        policy = lowPower || lowData ? .frugal : .normal
    }
}
```

The `coalesceUpdates` value isn't only a client setting: send it to the server when subscribing ("at most one update every 2 seconds"), so the saving happens before the bytes reach the radio.

## Measuring it

You can't see radio cost on a Mac or in the Simulator, which uses your Mac's network. Measure on a device, on cellular:

- **Xcode's energy impact gauge** (in the debug navigator while the app runs) shows network and CPU overhead live; watch it with a socket idle for a few minutes.
- **MetricKit** reports what happened on real users' devices, in daily payloads: `MXNetworkTransferMetric` for bytes over Wi-Fi and cellular, and `MXCellularConditionMetric` for the signal strength they were on. A jump in cellular transfer after a release is a heartbeat or polling regression.
- **Xcode Organizer** shows battery usage metrics aggregated from users who share diagnostics, by app version.

You'll go deeper into energy, including CPU, location and thermal state, in D51.

## Trade-offs

| Approach | Freshness | Radio wake-ups | Wins when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Socket with a 10 s heartbeat | Dead connections found in seconds | Constant: the radio never sleeps | Almost never on cellular |
| Socket with a 25–30 s heartbeat, ping only when quiet | Live, failures found within ~40 s | A few per minute when idle | The foreground default |
| Socket with a 45 s heartbeat and coalesced updates | Live, a little slower to notice failure | About half as many | Low Power Mode, Low Data Mode |
| Polling every 30–60 s | Interval-bound | One per poll, plus the tail | Low-rate data, and when no socket is open anyway |
| Push, then fetch on open | Seconds, best-effort | Shared system connection | Everything in the background |

<Callout kind="interview">

**Say** the cost and the mitigation together: "Each heartbeat wakes the cellular radio for a tail of several seconds, so a 30-second heartbeat keeps it awake about a third of the time the socket is open. We only ping when the connection is quiet, share one socket across screens, batch receipts and analytics into existing wake-ups, coalesce high-frequency updates on the server, close the socket when nothing live is on screen, and stretch the heartbeat in Low Power Mode. Nothing stays connected in the background; that's what push is for." **Ask** how long users keep the live screen open; that's the number that turns the per-hour cost into a daily one. **Red flags:** counting bytes instead of wake-ups, sub-15-second heartbeats on cellular, and background sockets "for freshness".

</Callout>

<Callout kind="gotcha" title="iOS gotcha: the office Wi-Fi hides the cost">

Developers test on fast Wi-Fi, where wake-ups are cheap and the energy gauge stays low. The same app on cellular with one bar of signal can cost several times as much per hour, because a weak signal needs more transmit power and retransmissions. Before shipping a heartbeat or polling change, leave the app connected on cellular for twenty minutes and look at the gauge and at Settings → Battery.

</Callout>
