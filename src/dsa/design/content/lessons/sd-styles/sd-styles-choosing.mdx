## Why it matters

You now know seven styles, and every one of them is the right answer somewhere. What separates a senior answer from a junior one is not knowing more styles; it's choosing **from the qualities the system needs**, saying what the choice gives up, and knowing what would change your mind. Interviewers score exactly that sentence: "Because X and Y matter most here, I'd use S, accepting Z."

## Start from the qualities, not the style

Every style buys some qualities with others. So the first step is deciding which qualities matter most for *this* system. Mark Richards and Neal Ford call these the driving **architecture characteristics**, and advise picking no more than about three: if everything is a priority, nothing is.

They come from three places:

- **Explicit requirements:** "inspectors work 8 hours with no signal", "the feed opens in under a second".
- **Implicit ones nobody writes down:** security for anything with money, accessibility, not losing a user's work.
- **Constraints:** team size and structure, deadline, regulation, the platform (one binary through App Review, background limits, battery), and the codebase you already have.

Turn each into something measurable, as in D01: "offline" becomes "create, edit and attach photos to 30 inspections with no network, and sync them within 5 minutes of reconnecting". A quality you can't measure can't decide anything.

## What each style tends to give

These are tendencies, not laws: a well-built event-driven app can be traceable, and a layered app can be a mess. Use the table to see where each style pulls.

| Style | Simple | Testable | Evolvable | Fast | Scales out | Isolates faults | Traceable | Team autonomy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Layered | High | Med | Med | High | Low | Low | High | Low |
| Event-driven | Low | Med | High | High | High | Med | Low | High |
| Pipes and filters | High | High | Med | High | High | Low | High | Med |
| Microkernel | Med | High | High | Med | Low | Low (High for extensions) | Med | High |
| Modular monolith | Med | High | High | High | Med | Low | High | Med |
| Distributed | Low | Med | High | Low | High | High | Low | High |
| Hexagonal / clean | Low | High | High | High | n/a | Low | High | Med |

Read it by column. If **traceability** is a driving quality (a banking app that must explain every transfer), the event-driven and distributed rows are warnings. If **team autonomy** is (40 engineers in six squads), the layered row is.

## Choose at each scale

One app has several architectural decisions, and each can use a different style. Decide them separately, top down:

1. **System:** where the app sits on the thin-to-thick client dial, and the backend's shape.
2. **App structure:** layers, feature modules, plug-in points.
3. **Feature:** which member of the model–view family.
4. **Components:** a pipeline for images, an event bus for analytics, a registry for card types.

Three apps, three sets of answers:

| | Banking app | Photo editor | Super-app, 15 squads |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Driving qualities | Security, correctness, traceability | Performance, extensibility, offline | Team autonomy, build time, release cadence |
| System | Balanced client; every money rule on the server | Thick client; the server is only for sync and sharing | Balanced client plus server-driven content |
| App structure | Modular monolith, strict layers in payments | Modular monolith | Modular monolith with interface modules, mini-apps as plug-ins |
| Inside | Direct `async` calls for money flows, events only for analytics | Pipes and filters for rendering, plug-ins for tools, a unidirectional store for edit history | Each squad chooses within agreed rules |
| Gave up | Some speed of change | Simplicity | Consistency between squads; more tooling |

## Making "it depends" concrete

When two options are close, score them. A weighted decision matrix (D01 covered [decision matrices](/design/learn/sd-quality-decisions)) makes the trade visible and, more usefully, shows **how sensitive** the answer is to the weights.

```swift run title="A weighted decision matrix, before and after the team grows"
// Each option is scored 1 (poor) to 5 (strong) on, in order:
// offline, testability, team autonomy, simplicity, time to ship
let options: [(name: String, scores: [Int])] = [
    ("Layered app, one target", [4, 3, 2, 5, 5]),
    ("Modular monolith (feature packages)", [4, 4, 4, 3, 3]),
    ("Micro-apps on an event bus", [3, 3, 5, 1, 2]),
]

func rank(weights: [Int]) {
    let totals = options.map { option in
        (option.name, zip(weights, option.scores).map { $0 * $1 }.reduce(0, +))
    }
    for (name, total) in totals.sorted(by: { $0.1 > $1.1 || ($0.1 == $1.1 && $0.0 < $1.0) }) {
        print("  \(total)  \(name)")
    }
}

// Five engineers, one product: shipping fast and keeping it simple matter most
print("A team of 5:")
rank(weights: [5, 4, 1, 4, 5])

// Forty engineers in six squads: autonomy and testability now outweigh simplicity
print("A team of 40:")
rank(weights: [5, 5, 5, 2, 2])
```

```output
A team of 5:
  79  Layered app, one target
  67  Modular monolith (feature packages)
  46  Micro-apps on an event bus
A team of 40:
  72  Modular monolith (feature packages)
  65  Layered app, one target
  61  Micro-apps on an event bus
```

The matrix didn't make the decision; the weights did. What it gives you is the argument: "with five of us, the single target wins comfortably; at around 40 engineers the modular monolith overtakes it, so we should modularise before we hire that far". Try your own weights:

```visual estimator
title: Weigh the qualities, watch the ranking move
inputs:
  - { id: wOffline, label: Weight for offline, min: 0, max: 5, step: 1, value: 5 }
  - { id: wTest, label: Weight for testability, min: 0, max: 5, step: 1, value: 4 }
  - { id: wTeam, label: Weight for team autonomy, min: 0, max: 5, step: 1, value: 1 }
  - { id: wSimple, label: Weight for simplicity, min: 0, max: 5, step: 1, value: 4 }
  - { id: wShip, label: Weight for time to ship, min: 0, max: 5, step: 1, value: 5 }
outputs:
  - { id: layered, label: "Layered app, one target", formula: wOffline * 4 + wTest * 3 + wTeam * 2 + wSimple * 5 + wShip * 5 }
  - { id: modular, label: Modular monolith (feature packages), formula: wOffline * 4 + wTest * 4 + wTeam * 4 + wSimple * 3 + wShip * 3 }
  - { id: micro, label: Micro-apps on an event bus, formula: wOffline * 3 + wTest * 3 + wTeam * 5 + wSimple * 1 + wShip * 2 }
```

Notice how hard it is to make the micro-apps option win: it only comes first when team autonomy outweighs nearly everything else and simplicity and time to ship barely count. When an option loses under every plausible weighting, drop it and say why.

## Write it down, and protect it

A style choice is only as good as its record and its enforcement.

- **Record the decision:** the context, the options, what you chose, what you gave up and **what would make you revisit it** ("if a second team starts on the editor, split it into its own module"). That's an architecture decision record; D09 has a first look, and D31 goes further.
- **Protect it with fitness functions:** a CI check on module imports (like the one in [What an architecture style is](/design/learn/sd-styles-intro)), a launch-time budget, a test that the domain module builds without UIKit. Unchecked constraints decay.
- **Expect to evolve.** The right style for a team of five is often wrong for fifty. A modular monolith with clean seams is the shape that's cheapest to change later, which is why it's the default answer for so many apps.

## Trade-offs

| Approach to choosing | Quick | Defensible | Adapts as things change | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "What we used last time" | Yes | No | No | The last system's qualities aren't this one's |
| The fashionable style | Yes | Weakly | No | Paying for qualities you don't need |
| Qualities first, per scale | Medium | Yes | Yes, with recorded triggers | Over-analysis, if you weigh 20 qualities |
| Full weighted matrix | Slow | Very | Yes | False precision: the scores are judgements |

Qualities-first is the everyday method. Bring out the full matrix when two options are close, or when you need to convince people who disagree.

<Callout kind="interview">

**Say it in one breath:** "The driving qualities are offline use and testability, and the team will triple, so: a thick client for drafts, a modular monolith with feature packages, MVVM per screen, and events only for analytics. I'm giving up some simplicity now; if the team stays small I'd collapse the modules."

**Ask:** the questions that move the weights: how many engineers, how long the app must live, what must work offline, what's regulated.

**Red flags:** choosing before asking; one style at every scale; no cost named; "it depends" without saying on what.

</Callout>

<Callout kind="gotcha" title="iOS gotcha: the platform makes some choices for you">

Some qualities are fixed by iOS whatever style you prefer. The app is one binary, so features can't deploy separately. Background execution is limited and scheduled by the system, so "a background service that syncs every minute" isn't available; design around `BGTaskScheduler`, push and foreground sync. Extensions are separate processes with tight memory limits. Name these constraints early; they rule options out before any weighting starts.

</Callout>

## Practice

Two items put this lesson to work. The design exercise asks you to choose and justify the styles for an offline field-inspection app, at every scale. The review gives you a written design whose style fights the qualities its own document lists, along with the code it produced.

```cards
- id: top-three
  front: "How many driving qualities should a style decision rest on?"
  back: "About three. They come from explicit requirements, implicit ones (security, not losing work) and constraints (team, deadline, platform), each made measurable."
- id: per-scale
  front: "At which scales do you choose a style?"
  back: "System (client dial, backend shape), app structure (layers, modules, plug-ins), feature (model–view pattern) and components (pipelines, events, registries). Each can differ."
- id: matrix-value
  front: "What does a weighted decision matrix really give you?"
  back: "Not the answer but the argument: which weights decide it, how sensitive the ranking is, and which options lose under every plausible weighting."
- id: record-trigger
  front: "What must a recorded style decision include besides the choice?"
  back: "What was given up, and the trigger that would make you revisit it, such as team size, load or a new requirement."
```

```quiz
- id: q1
  type: mcq
  prompt: "A hospital app records medication given to patients. Auditors must be able to reconstruct who did what, and in what order. Which style is the worst fit for the medication flow itself?"
  options:
    - "Strict layering with direct calls"
    - "A broker-style event chain, where each step reacts to the previous step's event"
    - "A hexagonal core with ports for storage and the hospital's API"
    - "A modular monolith"
  answer: 1
  explain: "Traceability is a driving quality, and a broker-style chain makes the flow emerge from subscribers, so no single place shows the order of events. Events can still record an audit trail, but the flow should be explicit."
- id: q2
  type: order
  prompt: "Put the steps of choosing a style in order."
  lines:
    - "Find the top three qualities, from requirements and constraints"
    - "Make each one measurable"
    - "Choose a style at each scale, starting from the system"
    - "Score close options against weighted qualities"
    - "Record the choice, what it gives up and what would change it"
    - "Add fitness functions that keep the constraints true"
  explain: "Qualities come before styles, decisions are made per scale, and a decision isn't finished until it's recorded and protected."
- id: q3
  type: multi
  prompt: "Which of these should change your choice of style?"
  options:
    - "The team will grow from 6 to 30 engineers next year"
    - "The app must work for a day without signal"
    - "The tech lead enjoyed a conference talk on micro-frontends"
    - "Regulators require an audit trail of every change to a record"
    - "The app ships as one binary through App Review"
  answer: [0, 1, 3, 4]
  explain: "Team size, offline needs, regulation and platform constraints all shift the qualities that matter. Enthusiasm for a style isn't a quality the system needs."
- id: q4
  type: mcq
  prompt: "In the matrix above, which change in weights would most help the micro-apps option?"
  options:
    - "More weight on simplicity"
    - "More weight on time to ship"
    - "More weight on team autonomy, and none on simplicity or time to ship"
    - "More weight on offline"
  answer: 2
  explain: "Micro-apps score best only on team autonomy (5) and worst on simplicity (1) and time to ship (2), so that change helps them most. Even then the modular monolith stays ahead unless offline and testability barely count either, which is itself the argument."
- id: q5
  type: recall
  prompt: "A photo-editing app must render previews at 60 fps, work fully offline, and let a partner team add new filters. In three or four sentences, choose styles at two or more scales and say what you give up."
  model: "Thick client: everything works on the device, and the server only syncs and shares. The render path is a pipes-and-filters pipeline (Core Image or Metal), so filters are independent stages and the slowest one can be found and optimised. Filters and tools are plug-ins behind a small contract and a registry, so the partner team adds filters without touching the editor core; edit history lives in a unidirectional store, which makes undo and redo straightforward. I'm giving up simplicity and some compile-time checking (unknown filter kinds need a fallback), which I'd revisit if only our team ever added filters."
```
