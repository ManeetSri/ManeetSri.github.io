## Why it matters

Two apps can do exactly the same things and need completely different designs. A notes app that must work on a plane needs a local database and sync; one that must open in under half a second needs a cache-first launch; one that must be end-to-end encrypted can't let the server search your notes. The features are identical. What differs is how *well* each must work, and under which conditions, and that's what decides the architecture. Interviewers open every design question by checking whether you find those requirements before you draw anything.

## Three kinds of requirement

When someone describes what they want built, everything they say falls into one of three buckets.

**Functional requirements** say what the system *does*: its behaviour, from input to output. "A rider can buy a single ticket." "Pull to refresh reloads the feed." "A message shows a tick when it's delivered." You can usually write them as user stories, and each one tends to become a feature, a screen or an endpoint.

**Non-functional requirements** say how *well* it does it, and under which conditions. "The feed shows posts within a second of launch, even with no signal." "A stolen phone can't be used to read someone's messages." These are also called **quality attributes**, and they're the subject of this whole module.

**Constraints** are decisions already made for you. "Supports iOS 17 and later." "Must sign in with the company's SSO." "Ships in March with a team of three." "No new third-party SDKs without legal review." You don't trade constraints off against anything; they take options off the table before you start.

<Callout kind="analogy">

Building a house: the functional requirements are the rooms (a kitchen, two bedrooms, a bathroom). The quality attributes are how warm it stays in winter, how quiet the bedrooms are and how long the roof lasts. The constraints are the plot, the budget and the planning rules. Two houses with the same rooms can be built completely differently, and it's the warmth, the noise and the budget that decide how.

</Callout>

Here's one product, a messaging app, sorted into the three buckets:

| Functional | Quality attribute | Constraint |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Send a text or photo to a contact | A sent message appears on the other phone within 2 s at p95 | Supports iOS 17+ |
| See when a message was read | Messages written offline are sent when the connection returns, in order | Push goes through APNs |
| Search old conversations | Only the two participants can read a message, not the server | Two iOS engineers |
| Mute a conversation | The app uses under 2% battery an hour in the background | Must ship before the summer |

The line between buckets can blur. "Works offline" sounds like a feature, but it's really a quality (availability under a network condition) that touches every feature. A useful test: if removing it would delete a screen or an endpoint, it's functional; if removing it would change how many parts you need, or how they talk, it's a quality attribute.

## Why quality attributes shape the architecture

Functional requirements mostly *add* code: a new screen, a new endpoint. Quality attributes change the *structure*: which parts exist, where data lives and how the parts talk. Take one functional requirement, "show my messages", and add a single quality attribute each time:

| Add this quality | And the design gains |
| --- | --- |
| Opens in under 300 ms | A local cache read at launch, before any network call |
| Works on a plane | A local database, an outbox for unsent messages and a sync protocol |
| End-to-end encrypted | Keys generated and kept on the device; a server that stores only ciphertext and can't search |
| Survives 10× more users next year | Pagination everywhere and a server that can scale out |

That's why architects talk about **architecturally significant requirements**: the handful of requirements that would be expensive to change after the design is built. They're almost always quality attributes. Adding a "mute" button late is cheap; adding offline support to an app designed to fetch everything on demand is a rewrite.

Mobile adds qualities a backend engineer rarely thinks about: battery and data use, app size, cold launch time, behaviour on a phone that's three years old, and the fact that old versions of your app keep running for years because you can't force everyone to update.

## From adjectives to testable requirements

Product briefs are full of adjectives: *fast, secure, reliable, intuitive, scalable*. They're a starting point, not a requirement, because nobody can check them. A testable quality requirement has a **metric**, a **threshold**, the **conditions** it holds under and, for anything that varies, a **percentile**.

| Vague | Testable |
| --- | --- |
| The app is fast | Cold launch to first content under 1 s at p90 on an iPhone 12 |
| Scrolling is smooth | The feed scrolls with no hitches over 50 ms on a 120 Hz device |
| It's reliable | 99.8% of sessions are crash-free, measured over 7 days |
| It works offline | Every screen shows its last data with no network; actions queue and send within 30 s of reconnecting |
| It's secure | Tokens live in the Keychain; a backup of the phone contains no credentials |
| It scales | The API serves 5,000 requests a second at peak with p99 under 300 ms |

To get from the left column to the right, ask four questions: **who** is affected, under **what conditions**, **how much** is good enough, and **how will we know**? Percentiles matter because averages hide the people having a bad time: if the mean launch is 400 ms but one launch in ten takes 3 s, a tenth of your users think the app is slow. You'll work with percentiles properly in the performance lesson later in this module.

## Quality-attribute scenarios

The most useful format for a quality requirement comes from the Software Engineering Institute (SEI), in Bass, Clements and Kazman's *Software Architecture in Practice*. A **quality-attribute scenario** has six parts:

1. **Source:** who or what causes the event (a user, an attacker, the OS, a server).
2. **Stimulus:** the event itself (opens the app, a request arrives, a disk fills up).
3. **Artefact:** the part of the system that's stressed (the whole app, the feed screen, the sync engine).
4. **Environment:** the conditions at the time (offline, cold launch, peak load, low battery).
5. **Response:** what the system should do.
6. **Response measure:** the number that tells you whether it did.

Step through one for a news app's feed:

```visual flow
title: One quality-attribute scenario, part by part
nodes:
  - { id: source, label: Commuter, kind: actor, at: [0, 0] }
  - { id: feed, label: Feed screen, kind: view, at: [2, 0] }
  - { id: response, label: Cached posts, kind: store, at: [4, 0] }
  - { id: measure, label: "≤ 1 s at p90", kind: service, at: [4, 2] }
groups:
  - { id: env, label: "No signal, cold launch", nodes: [feed, response] }
edges:
  - [source, feed, opens app]
  - [feed, response, renders]
  - [response, measure, timed, { style: data }]
steps:
  - say: "**Source:** a commuter. Naming who causes the event tells you whose expectations you're meeting."
    active: [source]
  - say: "**Stimulus:** they open the app. The event is specific: a cold launch, not \"using the app\"."
    send: source->feed
  - say: "**Artefact:** the feed screen is the part under stress. Sometimes it's the whole app; sometimes one module, such as the sync engine."
    active: [feed]
  - say: "**Environment:** on a train, with no signal, and the app not in memory. The same stimulus on Wi-Fi is a different, easier scenario."
    active: [env]
  - say: "**Response:** show the last cached posts with an \"offline\" label, rather than a spinner."
    send: feed->response
    state: { response: ok }
  - say: "**Response measure:** within 1 s at p90 on an iPhone 12. Without a number, nobody can say whether the design meets it."
    send: response->measure
    state: { measure: ok }
```

Written as a sentence: *when a commuter opens the app on a train with no signal, the feed screen shows the last cached posts within 1 s at p90 on an iPhone 12.* One sentence, and it already tells you there's a disk cache, that it's read before the network, and that someone has to measure launch time on an older phone.

Scenarios are plain data, so some teams keep them in the repository next to the tests that check them. A tiny model makes the missing piece in a weak one easy to spot:

```swift run
struct QualityScenario {
    let attribute: String
    let source: String
    let stimulus: String
    let environment: String
    let artefact: String
    let response: String
    let measure: String

    var sentence: String {
        "When \(source) \(stimulus) \(environment), the \(artefact) \(response) \(measure)."
    }

    // A crude check, but it catches the most common gap: a measure with no number in it
    var hasNumber: Bool { measure.contains(where: \.isNumber) }
}

let scenarios = [
    QualityScenario(attribute: "Performance", source: "a commuter", stimulus: "opens the app",
                    environment: "on a train with no signal", artefact: "feed screen",
                    response: "shows the last cached posts", measure: "within 1 s at p90 on an iPhone 12"),
    QualityScenario(attribute: "Usability", source: "a new user", stimulus: "signs up",
                    environment: "for the first time", artefact: "onboarding flow",
                    response: "feels quick and easy", measure: "for most people"),
]

for scenario in scenarios {
    print("\(scenario.attribute): \(scenario.sentence)")
    print(scenario.hasNumber ? "  testable" : "  not testable yet: the measure has no number")
}
```

```output
Performance: When a commuter opens the app on a train with no signal, the feed screen shows the last cached posts within 1 s at p90 on an iPhone 12.
  testable
Usability: When a new user signs up for the first time, the onboarding flow feels quick and easy for most people.
  not testable yet: the measure has no number
```

The usability scenario isn't wrong, it's unfinished. "Feels quick and easy for most people" becomes "80% of first-time users finish sign-up in under 60 seconds in a usability test", and now design choices (fewer fields, Sign in with Apple, deferring permissions) can be judged against it.

### Prioritising them

You'll always have more scenarios than you can design for equally. The SEI's **utility tree** rates each one on two scales, *business importance* and *technical difficulty*, from high to low. The scenarios rated high on both are where your design time goes; high importance with low difficulty needs care but little invention; low importance with high difficulty is the place to push back. In the [design framework](/design/learn/sd-orientation-framework) from D00, this is the moment between listing requirements and drawing the high-level design.

## Finding them in an interview

Interviewers rarely state the quality attributes. They give you a product ("design a photo-sharing app") and wait to see whether you ask. These questions find most of them:

- **Scale:** how many users, how many items each, how often? Mostly reading or mostly writing?
- **Conditions:** does it need to work offline or on poor networks? Which devices and OS versions?
- **Speed and freshness:** what must feel instant? How stale can data be?
- **Trust:** what's sensitive? Who must never see it? Any legal requirements (accessibility law, data residency)?
- **Priority:** if we can only make one of these excellent, which one?

Then write the three to five that matter most where everyone can see them, and refer back to them when you choose between options. "I'm putting the cache first because we said the feed opens in a second underground" is the sentence that shows a requirement driving a design.

## Trade-offs

How precisely you pin down a quality requirement is itself a trade-off:

| Approach | Effort | Can you test it? | Drives design decisions? | When it wins |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Adjectives ("fast, secure") | None | No | Barely: everyone reads them differently | Never past the first conversation |
| Measurable targets ("p90 launch under 1 s") | Low | Yes | Yes, for that metric | Most features: a handful of targets on the qualities that matter |
| Full six-part scenarios | Medium | Yes, including the conditions | Strongly: the environment exposes the hard cases | Architecturally significant requirements and anything safety- or money-critical |
| Formal specifications and SLAs | High | Yes, contractually | Strongly, but slowly | Regulated systems, or a service other teams depend on |

<Callout kind="interview">

**Say:** "Before I design anything, let me pin down the non-functional requirements, because they'll decide the architecture." Then ask about scale, offline, latency, freshness, security and priorities, and write the answers down as numbers: "so, feed in a second on a cold launch, works offline, about 10 million daily users."

**Ask:** "Which matters most if they conflict: freshness or working offline?" That question shows you know they will.

**Red flags:** jumping straight to boxes and arrows; listing quality attributes as adjectives and never using them again; promising every quality at once ("it'll be fast, secure, offline and real-time") without noticing the tension.

</Callout>

<Callout kind="gotcha" title="iOS gotcha: requirements the platform won't let you meet">

Some quality requirements sound reasonable and are impossible on iOS. "Refresh the data every 15 minutes in the background" can't be promised: `BGAppRefreshTask` runs when the system decides, based on how the person uses the app, battery and network, and may not run at all for days. Silent pushes are throttled too. If a requirement depends on background execution, rewrite it around what you control ("data is never more than 15 minutes old **when the app is opened**"), and check every quality requirement against the platform's rules before you agree to it: the App Review Guidelines, privacy manifests and background limits are constraints you inherit.

</Callout>

## Practice

The design exercise for this lesson, *Quality scenarios for a transit ticket*, gives you a brief that says only "fast, secure, always available and accessible". Write at least five six-part scenarios with real numbers, rank them, and then let them drive the design. Compare your scenarios with the model answer before you look at its architecture: the scenarios are the point.
